1.7.2 The Modelling Workflow

From Words to a Decision

  1. Understand the question. Identify what is known and what must be found.
  2. Define variables. Include a meaning and unit for every unknown.
  3. State assumptions. Decide what is being simplified or treated as exact.
  4. Write constraints. Translate each relationship into an equation.
  5. Build the matrices. Keep one consistent variable order.
  6. Solve. Use an appropriate numerical method.
  7. Verify. Check A @ x against b.
  8. Interpret. Attach names and units to the numbers.
  9. Evaluate. Decide whether the answer is reasonable and usable.

Do Not Start with the Matrix

First define the variables and equations. A matrix entered without a clear variable order may be numerically valid but represent the wrong problem.

1.7.3 A General Applied Solver

def solve_model(A, b, variable_names):
    """Solve A @ x = b, verify it, and label the result."""
    A = np.array(A, dtype=float)
    b = np.array(b, dtype=float)

    if A.shape[0] != A.shape[1]:
        raise ValueError("This helper expects a square system.")
    if A.shape[1] != len(variable_names):
        raise ValueError("Provide one name for each variable.")

    x = np.linalg.solve(A, b)
    verified = np.allclose(A @ x, b)

    result = dict(zip(variable_names, x))
    return result, verified

result, verified = solve_model(
    [[2, 1], [1, 3]],
    [5, 5],
    ["x", "y"]
)

print(result)
print("Verified:", verified)

Labels reduce the risk of interpreting the solution entries in the wrong order.

1.7.4 Application 1: Ticket Sales

A fundraiser sells student tickets for $6 and general tickets for $10. A total of 80 tickets produces $620 in revenue.

Step 1: Define Variables

  • s = number of student tickets;
  • g = number of general tickets.

Step 2: Form the Equations

s + g = 80
6s + 10g = 620

Step 3: Solve

A = np.array([[1, 1], [6, 10]], dtype=float)
b = np.array([80, 620], dtype=float)

solution = np.linalg.solve(A, b)
s, g = solution

print("Student tickets:", s)
print("General tickets:", g)
print("Verified:", np.allclose(A @ solution, b))

Step 4: Interpret

The fundraiser sold 45 student tickets and 35 general tickets.

Context Check

Ticket counts must be nonnegative whole numbers. The computed values meet both requirements, add to 80, and reproduce $620.

1.7.5 Application 2: Mixing Concentrations

How many litres of a 20% solution and a 50% solution should be combined to create 30 L of a 32% solution?

Variables

  • x = litres of 20% solution;
  • y = litres of 50% solution.

Conservation Equations

x + y = 30
0.20x + 0.50y = 0.32(30) 
A = np.array([[1, 1], [0.20, 0.50]], dtype=float)
b = np.array([30, 0.32 * 30], dtype=float)

x, y = np.linalg.solve(A, b)

print("20% solution:", x, "L")
print("50% solution:", y, "L")
print("Final amount:", x + y, "L")
print("Pure component:", 0.20*x + 0.50*y, "L")

The mixture requires 18 L of the 20% solution and 12 L of the 50% solution.

Percentages Must Be Decimals

Use 0.20, not 20, when 20% is used as a coefficient.

1.7.6 Application 3: Resource Allocation

A workshop produces products A and B. Each unit uses the following resources:

ResourceProduct AProduct BAvailable
Machine time2 hours1 hour100 hours
Labour1 hour3 hours120 hours

Assume all available hours are used. Let a and b be the numbers of units of products A and B.

2a + b = 100
a + 3b = 120 
A = np.array([[2, 1], [1, 3]], dtype=float)
resources = np.array([100, 120], dtype=float)

production = np.linalg.solve(A, resources)
a, b = production

print("Product A:", a)
print("Product B:", b)
print("Resources used:", A @ production)

The model gives 36 units of product A and 28 units of product B.

Equation or Inequality?

The equations assume both resources are used completely. If the workshop may leave resources unused, the natural constraints are inequalities and the problem becomes an optimization model rather than only a linear system.

1.7.7 Application 4: Nutrition Planning

Food A supplies 10 g of protein and 5 g of carbohydrates per serving. Food B supplies 5 g of protein and 15 g of carbohydrates. Find servings that provide exactly 50 g of protein and 60 g of carbohydrates.

A = np.array([
    [10,  5],
    [ 5, 15]
], dtype=float)

targets = np.array([50, 60], dtype=float)
servings = np.linalg.solve(A, targets)

print("Food A servings:", servings[0])
print("Food B servings:", servings[1])
print("Nutrients supplied:", A @ servings)

The result is 3.6 servings of Food A and 2.8 servings of Food B.

Interpret Fractional Servings Carefully

A fractional value may be practical for food quantities but not for indivisible objects. The context determines whether rounding is acceptable.

1.7.8 Application 5: Routing Through a Network

A network sends 700 data packets through two routes. Route 1 has a cost of 20 units per packet and Route 2 has a cost of 35 units per packet. The total routing cost is 18,500 units.

Let x and y be the numbers of packets sent through Routes 1 and 2.

x + y = 700
20x + 35y = 18,500 
A = np.array([[1, 1], [20, 35]], dtype=float)
b = np.array([700, 18500], dtype=float)

routes = np.linalg.solve(A, b)
x, y = routes

print("Route 1 packets:", x)
print("Route 2 packets:", y)
print("Verified:", np.allclose(A @ routes, b))

The network sends 400 packets through Route 1 and 300 through Route 2.

1.7.9 Application 6: Traffic Flow Conservation

At a simplified junction, 420 vehicles per hour enter from the west and 180 enter from the north. Traffic exits along two roads. The eastern exit carries 100 more vehicles per hour than the southern exit.

Let e and s denote the eastern and southern exit flows.

e + s = 600
e − s = 100 
A = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]], dtype=float)
b = np.array([600, 100], dtype=float)

e, s = np.linalg.solve(A, b)

print("East:", e, "vehicles/hour")
print("South:", s, "vehicles/hour")

The eastern flow is 350 vehicles per hour and the southern flow is 250 vehicles per hour.

Conservation Principle

In a steady-flow model with no accumulation, total inflow equals total outflow. Real traffic may vary with time, so this is a simplified snapshot.

1.7.10 Application 7: Comparing Service Plans

Plan A costs $30 plus $4 per use. Plan B costs $54 plus $2 per use. At how many uses do the plans cost the same, and what is that cost?

Let u be the number of uses and c the common cost.

−4u + c = 30
−2u + c = 54 
A = np.array([[-4, 1], [-2, 1]], dtype=float)
b = np.array([30, 54], dtype=float)

u, c = np.linalg.solve(A, b)

print("Equal at", u, "uses")
print("Common cost: $", c)

The plans cost the same at 12 uses, when each costs $78.

1.7.11 Validation Beyond np.allclose()

Numerical verification checks the equations, but an applied answer needs additional checks.

CheckQuestion
UnitsDoes each result have the correct unit?
SignMust the variables be nonnegative?
IntegralityMust counts be whole numbers?
CapacityDoes the solution exceed a physical or stated limit?
ScaleIs the magnitude plausible?
AssumptionsDoes the situation reasonably satisfy the model assumptions?
SensitivityWould a small change in the data cause a large change in the answer?
 
def model_checks(A, x, b):
    residual = A @ x - b
    return {
        "equations_verified": np.allclose(A @ x, b),
        "residual_norm": np.linalg.norm(residual),
        "all_nonnegative": np.all(x >= 0)
    }

1.7.12 Rounding and Discrete Decisions

Rounding each variable independently can destroy the equations. Suppose a solver returns counts such as 12.4 and 7.6. Rounding both values may change totals or resource use.

Do Not Round Before Verification

Keep full numerical precision during calculations. If whole-number decisions are required, verify the rounded values separately. Some problems require integer optimization rather than ordinary linear-system solving.

rounded = np.rint(solution).astype(int)

print("Rounded candidate:", rounded)
print("Still satisfies model:", np.allclose(A @ rounded, b))

1.7.13 Model Assumptions and Limitations

A linear model assumes that contributions combine through addition and constant multiples. Common assumptions include:

  • unit prices, concentrations, or resource rates are constant;
  • quantities are measured without important error;
  • there are no discounts, thresholds, delays, or nonlinear effects;
  • every relevant constraint has been included; and
  • the system describes the time period or operating conditions of interest.

A correct computation answers the mathematical model. Whether it answers the real problem depends on the quality of these assumptions.

1.7.15 Common Modelling Errors

ProblemCauseCorrection
The equations use different variable ordersVariables were not defined before coefficients were recorded.Write the variable vector explicitly and keep that order.
Terms with incompatible units are addedUnits were ignored.Check every equation dimensionally.
A percentage is too largeFor example, 30% was entered as 30.Use the decimal 0.30.
The numerical solution is accepted immediatelyOnly the solver output was considered.Verify the equations and apply contextual checks.
Counts are fractionalA continuous model was used for discrete objects.Determine whether rounding is valid or a different model is needed.
An equality was used for a capacityThe model assumed all resources must be used.Use an inequality-based optimization model when unused capacity is allowed.

  1. Why should variables be defined with units?
  2. What is the difference between numerical verification and contextual validation?
  3. Why is nonnegativity important in many applied systems?
  4. Why can rounding a solution be dangerous?
  5. What assumption changes a resource inequality into an equality?
  6. What does a small residual norm tell you—and what does it not tell you?
Show suggested answers

  2. Verification checks the equations; validation checks whether the answer and assumptions make sense in reality.

Part 1 Summary

  • Applied modelling begins with clearly defined variables, units, and assumptions.
  • Verbal constraints can be organized into Ax = b.
  • NumPy can solve square, full-rank models and verify the resulting equations.
  • Residuals measure numerical discrepancy, not real-world validity.
  • Solutions must be interpreted and checked for signs, units, integrality, capacity, and plausibility.
  • Linear systems model sales, mixtures, resource use, nutrition, routing, traffic, and pricing.
  • A useful model is both mathematically correct and appropriate for its context.
